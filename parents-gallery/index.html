Parents, Abstracted

Parenthood is the oldest subject in art and one of the hardest to look at directly, so most of the works here hold two things at once: the abstraction that makes a parent legible as a shape — a body folded over a child, a hand around an ankle, two heads pressed into one silhouette — and the ordinary human being underneath it, tired, amused, or grieving.

The selection follows the same bias as the love gallery: works where the abstraction and the human survive together. It runs from the oldest formal images of a mother and child, through fathers, through artists painting their own parents, into the everyday labour of care and its grief. Every image is public domain or CC0, reproduced from Wikimedia Commons and stored locally beside this page so the gallery works offline. Each plate carries a short line from the King James Bible — never more than ten words — set into the image itself, and the lines are weighted towards the last section, grief, and the night. I

Madonna and Child

The mother-and-child pair is the oldest continuous image in Western art, and also the most formal: a seated body holding a smaller one, reduced to a single outline. Every painter here inherits the scheme and then humanises it from inside.

II

Fathers and Sons

Fatherhood is the harder subject, so painters arrive at it sideways: a hand held back, a banjo put into a boy's hands, a blessing given to the wrong son, a father absent from his own parable.

III

The Artist's Own Parents

The most direct approach, and the least polite one: artists painting the two people they came from, until portraiture stops being flattering and becomes a record.

IV

The Everyday Work of Care

Not the emblem but the labour: combing a child's hair, reading aloud for an hour, sitting in a garden, holding a child still long enough to finish.

V

Grief, and the Night

What parenthood costs. A mother holding a dead son, a bed, a lamp, a family lit from nowhere, and a room in which everyone sits apart.
